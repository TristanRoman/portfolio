AGAINST the Proposition

  • Applied 100% stacked design — visually normalizes totals, minimizing absolute differences. (Deceptive Score −0.5)

Final Reflection

This project examines how data visualization can be deliberately shaped to influence perception and strengthen or weaken a given argument. Using thousands of complaint filings from ProPublica’s Civilian Complaints Against New York City Police Officers dataset, I explored how subtle design and transformation choices—ranging from color and axis scaling to smoothing and categorical framing—can radically shift how an audience interprets the same underlying data. The goal was to understand how visuals can make a point feel “true” through design alone.

For the first visualization, “Amount of Police Injustice Claims Proven to Be True,” I employed a series of data and stylistic transformations that framed the story around growing accountability. I created a new smoothed variable using a three-year rolling average to remove short-term volatility and make the substantiation trend line appear more consistent over time. I then cherry-picked roughly the most recent eight years of data, excluding earlier periods where substantiation rates were lower—this gave the appearance of a clean, upward trajectory. To heighten this perception, I zoomed the y-axis to a tight range between roughly 0.33 and 0.40, which exaggerated minor improvements into dramatic visual jumps. Two subtle arrows were added along the trend to reinforce directionality, and minimal gridlines were used to keep attention on the movement rather than exact values. Through these transformations, the figure visually suggests meaningful progress, even though the underlying numerical change is relatively small. Each of these steps—annotation, smoothing, selective framing, and visual scaling—played a deliberate role in crafting a persuasive image of improvement.

For the second visualization, “Most Complaints Against Police Are Fraud,” I took the opposite approach, using similar data to convey an entirely different message. Here, I included the full historical range of records, allowing older data to flatten any recent upward trend, visually implying stagnation rather than progress. I transformed the multi-category disposition data into only two buckets—“Substantiated” and “All Other”—which simplified complexity and forced a binary interpretation. Using a 100% stacked-bar design, I normalized the totals so that every year appeared directly comparable, but this also minimized actual fluctuations in complaint volume. The color palette was intentionally chosen to emphasize “All Other” in a dominant maroon hue, while “Substantiated” was muted in gray, visually reinforcing the idea that nearly all complaints lack merit. To dramatize this further, I flipped the orientation of the chart from the previous one, so the large maroon blocks felt overwhelming and omnipresent. These design cues worked together to tell a story that most police complaints are exaggerated or false—an interpretation achieved not by falsifying data, but by altering how it was presented.

Through both visual narratives, I experimented with how color, form, structure, and context can all guide audience interpretation. In the “for” chart, I used motion, warmth, and tight framing to evoke progress and empathy. In the “against” chart, I used scale, dominance, and tonal contrast to provoke skepticism and defense. Both are persuasive—and both are manipulative in their own way.

These examples demonstrate how visual storytelling can both reveal and distort truth. Color, scale, orientation, and aggregation are powerful tools—when used responsibly, they can clarify complex realities; when abused, they can manipulate public understanding.

A real-world risk exists in how this very dataset could be misused. For instance, someone could extract officer identifiers, complaint dates, or incident locations and cross-reference them with other public records to identify individuals—potentially leading to doxxing, harassment, or retaliation. Conversely, insufficient anonymization could expose victims or witnesses, resulting in stigmatization or safety threats. Selectively framed visuals could also be exploited to justify biased policies or suppress oversight funding, misrepresenting systemic issues as evidence of public dishonesty rather than institutional failure.

Because such misuse is possible, ethical visualization requires more than good intent. Designers and analysts must document every transformation—timeframe selections, smoothing windows, and aggregation rules— and make this process transparent. Applying safeguards like minimum-group thresholds, contextual disclaimers, and open reproducibility helps prevent harm. Above all, data that challenges social norms or authority must be shared with honesty and humility, inviting scrutiny rather than silencing it. Visualizations can move people, but they should do so by illuminating truth—not weaponizing design.
